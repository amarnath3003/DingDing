Hector’s bell could say one thing: come here. Ding.AI’s bell can say anything.

People with ALS or locked-in syndrome often keep a sharp mind and exactly one reliable movement. Ding.AI turns that movement into whole sentences. It guesses what you want to say from the moment you are in, offers the guesses one at a time, and one ring says the chosen one aloud in your voice.

Spelling one word takes most of a minute.

With row-and-column scanning, each letter costs two presses and a wait while the highlight crawls to it. Spelling water on this grid takes 32 scan steps and 10 presses: about 38 seconds at a 1.2 s scan. By then the conversation has moved on.

Ding.AI doesn’t make you spell. When “I need water, please.” is the first guess, it takes one step and one press.

Built around one reliable movement.

The person using Ding.AI cannot use a mouse, touch or a keyboard. Their only output is one small movement that presses a desk call bell, and every press costs effort. So the screen never asks for more than that.

  • Every press is precious. Fewer, better options, the likeliest first.
  • Their words, their tone. Dry humour stays dry. Never childish, never clinical.
  • Nothing moves under their eyes. Options never reorder while they are being scanned.
  • Help is always one scan away. Three quick taps, from anywhere.

One bell, three gestures.

The highlight freezes the instant the bell goes down, so a press counts for the option that was lit when you reacted, not when the gesture finished.

Press

One tap chooses the highlighted option, and it is spoken aloud.

Hold

A second or more confirms the bigger moves: speak the draft, ask for “close, but…” variations, undo, go back.

Three quick taps

Help fires on the third tap, with no waiting. The caregiver presses “I’m coming” and Ding.AI says so out loud.

It reads the moment, then guesses.

Before each set of guesses the model sees who is waiting for an answer, what was just said, the time and the routine that is due, the room, any vitals that are off, and the face. Then the hub screens every card: no parroting the question, no asking for someone already in the room, no repeats.

Everything else it does, with the same bell.

Keeps up with conversation

When someone speaks, typed or heard by the microphone, every card becomes a reply to them. It answers what was asked and never repeats the question back.

A Hawking keyboard, rebuilt

For anything the guesses miss: sentence completions that even expand initials like “i w t g o”, next words and likeliest letters. Local prediction is instant; the AI’s lands about 1.5 s later.

Sounds like a person

Chosen words are spoken with OpenAI text-to-speech, cached and fetched ahead for the top cards, so a ring speaks instantly. Ding.AI’s own alerts use a separate voice.

Controls the room

“Turn the light on, please.” both says it and does it. A card may only switch a device if its own words mention it, so nothing happens that wasn’t said.

Learns the longer it’s used

Every pick is remembered with its moment. Ask again, even in different words, and the last answer comes back first, marked Learned. It is stored on that machine only.

Never runs out of options

If the AI is slow or unreachable, a hard deadline swaps in a local phrasebook and a 20,000-word predictor.

At night, it keeps watch.

The screen goes dark at nine and stays beside the bed. If something looks wrong and the bell stays silent, Ding.AI doesn’t wait to be asked.

A real desk bell, two wires and an ESP32.

The bell is an ordinary service bell. A switch between GPIO 4 and ground, with the internal pull-up on, is all the electronics there is. The ESP32 only sends debounced down and up edges over USB, and the hub classifies them with the same code as the Enter key.

Tuned on a real bell. A raw capture of a desk bell showed contact chatter of 30 to 300 ms and release chatter of 15 to 60 ms, so the hub debounces 3 ms on make and 30 ms on release.

Lesson learned. On a metal bell the frame connects the striker to the dome permanently, so the dome can’t be the switch. Detect the plunger with a microswitch instead, and the bell keeps ringing.

No bell yet? Enter is the bell. The behaviour is identical whichever you use.

Run it on your laptop.

Python for the hub, React for the screen. Add an OpenAI key, then open the Bell Screen on port 8000 and the operator panel at /#/sim in a second window.
